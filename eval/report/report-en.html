Routing vs Retrieval

Where a human-authored routing layer fails, and what it costs when it works. Chapters 1 and 2 of a measurement report.

corpus fingerprint a84ac6cf463a1a6d 700 questions, one frozen corpus arms rag · rag+rerank · routing · routing+overlay — all four a census models walks claude-sonnet-5 · rerank gpt-5 · embed text-embedding-3-large precondition mapcheck R1–R7 recorded with every run

The registered question is: how useful is human intervention in RAG, and where does it stop paying for itself? The intervention is a routing table — a partition of the corpus into areas, one sentence describing each, and a hierarchy beneath. An agent reads the table and decides where to look.

Chapter 1 asks when that stops working. Chapter 2 asks what it costs when it does work. The chapter that reports the winning numbers is written last, deliberately: every accuracy figure here favours routing, and a result reported only on the side it wins on is an advertisement.

1The moment human intervention fails

There are two ways a routing table can fail, and they are not the same failure.

IndicatorStatus
1 — the table is wrong, or stale without anyone meaning it tomeasured
2 — the table is entirely correct and still failsnot measured

1.1 Why this failure mode is the interesting one

Retrieval has no map. That is its weakness and, exactly, its immunity. A fusion retriever scores 0.028 on questions written in a person's words against documents indexed by codes — it does not degrade on those, it fails outright — but it cannot be misled, because there is nothing for it to believe.

A routing agent believes the table. That is where the gain comes from, and it is also the whole exposure. An agent that trusts the map inherits the map's errors, and inherits them silently.

1.2 Indicator 1 — one sentence, and the only miss in 700

Two documents in this corpus are forwarding notes: they sit in the area a subject moved out of and tell a walk where it went. Both said, of the page left behind:

They remain correct for anything dated before 2026-01-01 and for nothing after it.

False. That page is the oldest of three versions and stopped being correct at 2024-07-01 — eighteen months before the move the note describes. The note is not wrong about the move. It is silent about a revision that happened before it, and silence reads as nothing else to know.

One walk read the note, did exactly what it said, and answered a claim dated 2025 from the 2023 rule.

The part that proves it was the map, not the reader

Two routing arms ran. routing walks and reads; routing+overlay does the same while keeping an explicit working set. Both failed the same question, by the same two documents, in the same wrong era.

Two agents sharing no context, one of them carrying memory of its own decisions. A working set cannot help here, because nothing was forgotten. The note was read, understood, and obeyed.

Fixing the sentence fixed the walk. The note now carries the full version table and says outright that a question dated after 2024-07-01 is answered elsewhere — including dates before the move. Ten changed questions re-walked on the corrected corpus:

Same note, different destination. The agent did not become more careful. It was given a sentence that was true.

1.3 How far one wrong element reaches

The temptation is to say a bad routing table is far more dangerous than a bad document. The census supports a narrower version, and the narrow version is the defensible one.

One wrong elementreaches
a map row — procurement278 of 700 walks — 39.7%
a map row — top 20, median108 walks — 15.4%
a document — most retrieved106 of 700 lists — 15.1%
a document — median11 questions — 1.6%

About 2.6× at the top, not orders of magnitude. And the note that caused the miss lay on only 28 paths. So reach is the weakest part of the argument. Two things carry it instead:

  • A document competes; a map row is obeyed. A wrong document is one of ten candidates, weighed against nine others. A wrong sentence in the map is not weighed at all — it is the instruction the walk follows.
  • The failure hides. Retrieval's wrong answer looks wrong: thin, off-topic, absent. Routing's looks right — a specific figure, a named source, and a walk log that reads as a clean success. That is why this needed a 700-question census to surface: a 50-question sample drew the agent that went the other way and reported 1.000.

1.4 Indicator 2 — a correct table that stops working anyway

At this corpus, a correct map does not fail. 1,126 documents, 5 areas, 700 questions: 699 hits, and the single miss was a wrong map. Nothing here shows a limit.

That is not reassurance. It is one point on an axis, and a scaling limit cannot be seen from one point. The uncomfortable part is the asymmetry: retrieval's collapse is characterised and routing's is not, because scale was varied on one side only.

The only leading indicator visible today is a tail: eight walks in 700 (1.1%) took fifteen calls or more, and all eight hit. The median is flat at 6–7 across every family. A tail of successful long walks is what working, with effort looks like — consistent with being some distance before a limit, and not evidence of where the limit is.

1.5 Two checks that came out of this

The defect was found by a walk failing, which is expensive and ambiguous: a walk that goes wrong cannot tell you whether the map or the model was at fault. So both are now decided from the corpus, before any arm runs.

RuleWhat it refuses
mapcheck R6a forwarding note that names fewer dates than its subject's own revision legend
mapcheck R7a cycle in the cross-reference graph

R7 was written while the corpus passes — 993 references across 332 documents, no cycle — and verified by injecting one and watching it fail. It is the only rule in the set that no single sentence can be blamed for: payroll saying "for the rate, see attendance" and attendance saying "for how it is paid, see payroll" are both accurate, and there is nothing to correct except the structure.

A deliberate cycle was then built and walked. It cost nothing — 10/10 on both corpora, 5.5 calls per question on both. The agent went payroll → attendance, got what it came for, and never followed the back-pointer, because each edge carried a discriminator. The fixture was too easy, and saying so is better than filing a negative result as a finding about cycles in general. R7 stays: the harmless case and the harmful ones look identical in the graph, and the check runs before anyone knows which they have built.

Every accuracy figure above favours routing. None of them said what routing costs.

The reranker averages 0.56 calls rather than 1.0 because a question whose answer is not among the fused candidates is never sent to it — the miss is already decided. 390 of 700.

2.2 One number that will be quoted wrongly

Raw usage reports 200,000–500,000 input tokens per walk. That figure is real. Repeating it without qualification overstates the cost by an order of magnitude, because every tool call resends the conversation and almost all of it is served from cache.

Three numbers are available and two mislead. 500,000 overstates by an order of magnitude — it counts the same text many times. ~20 understates by three — it counts only what missed cache entirely. $0.12–$0.28 is measured, and it is the one this report uses.

2.3 The price above is the price when the map works

Count the tree: 128 nodes have something beneath them, 1,212 have a body. A full traversal — open every table, read every document — is 1,340 calls. At the measured 2,041 tokens of new content per call that is about 2.7M tokens, and no context holds it. The runner stops a walk at 40 turns, roughly 82k tokens, which is approximately a session rather than a setting waiting to be raised.

The table's job is not to save calls. It is to make the half a percent a walk can afford to look at be the right half a percent.

2.4 Which is the real difference between the arms

when its mechanism stops helping
retrievaldegrades — the answer slides down the ranking; a longer candidate list recovers some of it
routingdoes not degrade — the walk runs out of budget having seen 3% of the corpus and reports what it has

Retrieval has a dial with a known curve. Routing's equivalent dial is the turn ceiling, and it does not behave the same way: doubling it to 80 turns takes a walk from 3.0% of the tree to 6.0% — double the cost, and still 94% unseen, on a corpus where the answer is one document in 1,126.

This also explains the shape of the failure in Chapter 1. A walk misled by one sentence does not wander and recover. It goes somewhere plausible, answers the question it believes it was asked, and stops after six of a possible forty calls — with no mechanism that would make it spend the other thirty-four.

2.5 What the two chapters establish together

Retrieval collapses on a specific, nameable class of question — one written in a person's words against documents indexed by codes, or one asking about a version since replaced. On that class it does not degrade gracefully; it fails outright, at 0.028. Walking the table costs 20–100× more per question and fixes exactly that class.

And: neither arm has a graceful failure. They have different ungraceful ones — and only one of them looks like a success from the outside.

320 of these 700 questions are ones retrieval already answers first time, at 0.991, taken to 1.000 by one cheap reranking call. Nothing in this study argues for walking those. Which arm to use is a question about the mix of questions a system actually receives, and nothing here measures that mix for anyone but this corpus.

Limits

  • One corpus size. Indicator 2 is unmeasured; the scaling limit of a correct map is not known from one point.
  • n=5 for dollars and latency. One walk per lever, one run each. The shape is clear; no interval is quoted.
  • The reranker ran once. Every rag+rerank figure is a single draw with no variance estimate. The wide gaps survive any plausible wobble; the small claim that reranking helps does not, until it is repeated.
  • One model per arm. All walks are claude-sonnet-5.
  • Cost of authoring and maintaining the table is unmeasured. Stated as a cost, not quantified as one.
  • 1,340 is counted, not observed. No agent was made to attempt a full traversal; the number comes from the tree and the measured per-call tokens.
