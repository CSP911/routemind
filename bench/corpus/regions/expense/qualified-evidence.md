# Qualifying evidence

Which documents the tax office will accept, and what to do at each amount. The full list and the
amount table are on the page below.

## The shape of it
Four things qualify: a **tax invoice**, an **invoice**, a **card slip** (corporate or personal), and a
**cash receipt issued for business expense**. Everything else — a simple receipt, a handwritten note,
a statement of account, a deposit slip, a transfer record — does not, however convincing it looks.

## The cash receipt trap
A cash receipt can be issued two ways: against a personal identifier for the buyer's own year-end
deduction, or against the **company registration number** as a business expense. They look almost
identical on the paper. Only the second is usable here, and once issued the first way it cannot be
converted. **Say "사업자 지출증빙" and give the company number** before they ring it up.

## Amounts, in short
Up to 30,000 KRW a simple receipt is accepted. Over it, one of the four. Entertainment over 30,000 KRW
needs the attendee list as well. Condolence and congratulation payments up to 200,000 KRW are evidenced
by the invitation or the notice, which is the one place a non-standard document is the right one.

## What "the company pays a penalty" means in practice
Two percent of the amount, charged to the company, not to you. Nobody is billed for it personally — but
it is the reason the desk cannot simply wave a claim through, and it is why an argument about a 45,000
KRW simple receipt is not worth having. Getting the right paper at the counter takes ten seconds.
