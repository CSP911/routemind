# A superseded policy that was never named

A division head published a new vendor-selection rule in 2024. The proposal described the new process in full but never referenced the 2022 rule it was meant to replace.

## What went wrong

Both rules stayed on file as valid policy. The 2022 version was never withdrawn, so it remained in force alongside the 2024 one. A purchase checked against the old rule six months later passed; checked against the new one, it would have failed.

## What was missing

| Step missed | What it should have done |
|---|---|
| Naming what it replaces | Would have flagged the 2022 rule for withdrawal at the same time |
| Withdrawal filed | Would have closed the older rule's effective period cleanly |

## The trap

**A new policy does not automatically retire an old one just by covering the same ground.** Supersession has to be stated and the prior rule formally withdrawn — otherwise both are live, and whichever one a drafter happens to check becomes the answer, not the intended one.

This is the same gap a missing "what it replaces" field on the proposal form is meant to catch.
